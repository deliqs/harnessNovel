【Judge first, then rewrite】

Finish a “text-gene diagnosis” internally. Do not output the diagnosis:

- Judge the source’s register, narrative speed, sentence-length tendency, image sources, and dialogue habits.
- Find one or two original sentences that most represent the author’s voice. After refinement, that voice must still be there.
- Decide this chapter’s most needed problem, and revise only around the real problem.
- Judge whether the largest risk is loss of meaning, a register mismatch, broken rhythm, or over-literariness.

Then check the following problems paragraph by paragraph. Keep sentences with no problem as they are. Do not swap words sentence by sentence just to “change more”. Before every change, ask: is it truly more accurate and more natural than the original sentence? If it is not clearly better, do not change it.

1. Over-neatness: the same subject, the same sentence length, and the same “action + psychological explanation” structure used in a row.
2. Explaining for the reader: action and dialogue already made it clear, and the narration restates the character’s emotion, motive, or plot meaning once more.
3. Label emotions: writing “shocked, angry, complicated, unbelievable, sighing inwardly” with no perceptible reaction.
4. Over-polished: every action gets a metaphor, every turn is written as a film shot, every paragraph end wants an aphorism.
5. Model stock phrases: empty elevation, binary contrast, three-part parallelism, slogan tone, instruction-manual tone, mechanical suspense.
6. Dialogue too complete: characters in danger, argument, or probing still speak in full written logic, and dump all information in one go.
7. Rhythm chopped into a template: many stretches of “short sentence. Short sentence. A one-line conclusion on its own.”, with every action its own paragraph.
8. Rhythm stuck in a lump: one paragraph packed with action, observation, judgment, and result, with no natural pause.
9. Translationese: overusing “it is … that …”, “begin to …”, “attempt to …”, “has a …”, with time, state, and action stiffly separated.
10. Local body templates: relying again and again on fingertips, knuckles, palms, brow tips, lips, Adam’s apples, and pupils to carry every emotion.
11. Mechanical timing and counting: characters with no reason count how many seconds they stare, how many steps they walk, how many heartbeats, or repeatedly sense the same information.

【Fiction-writing rewrite rules】

I. Turn explanation into a present reaction

- Prefer action, pause, gaze, touch, sound, and choice the character can perceive to carry emotion.
- When the action already shows the emotion, delete the emotion label after it. Do not summarize for the reader again.
- Interior thought must stick to the situation in front of them. It may be short, obsessive, hesitant, or only half-thought. Avoid spectator-style analysis.
- Do not write every psychological change as a body reaction; heartbeat, clenched fists, shrinking pupils, and a sharp intake of breath must not be used over and over.
- Prefer whole posture, consequences of action, changes of choice, and environmental feedback to carry emotion. Do not shrink all emotion into fingers, eyebrows, and lips.
- Concrete states may be written directly. Piling detail to dodge plain lines such as “he froze” or “he cried” is usually faker than the original sentence.

II. Let narration breathe naturally

- Mix long and short sentences, but grammar must stay natural. Short sentences are only for landings, danger, discoveries, and decisions. Do not mechanically add one to every paragraph.
- A continuous action may be written together; start a new paragraph when the action’s direction changes, information is revealed, or emotion turns.
- Word order must follow natural English. Do not manufacture inverted fragments for the sake of variation, such as “all sweat, the palms”, “slightly burning, the jade plate”, “beginning to change color, the flames”.
- Put back components that were forcibly split, such as “action. Location.” and “conclusion. Reason.” If it can be said naturally as one sentence, do not use a period to fake atmosphere.
- Delete filter words that do no work. Change “he began to walk toward the door” and “he attempted to grab the sword hilt” into the action that actually happens; if “began / attempted” carries unfinished or failed meaning, it must be kept.
- Fix literal “it is … that …” structures and split verb phrases. For example, change “It was after half a shichen that the bell rang” to “Half a shichen later, the bell rang.”
- A little ordinary repetition is allowed. Do not, in order to avoid repetition, mechanically replace “Yan Che” with “the youth, the young man, the protagonist, he”.
- Fix obvious grammar errors and article / preposition / tense mistakes. Do not deliberately manufacture typos, fragments, or punctuation errors to impersonate a human writer.
- Do not abuse stacked modifiers. When you need coordination, use commas, conjunctions, or split sentences by sense, but do not mechanically delete normal commas.

III. Make dialogue sound like a specific person speaking

- Modal particles already in the source, such as “ah, huh, right, well, oh”, are in principle kept; they belong to the character’s voice. Except for obvious typos, do not delete, replace, or add them on your own.
- From identity and situation, keep necessary pauses, rhetorical questions, omissions, and interruptions, but do not add a new modal particle to every line.
- In tension, speech may be short, off-center, or unfinished; a person in control of the scene may skip explanation. The information must still be clear.
- Do not, in order to manufacture subtext, cut one complete line of dialogue into many fragments. Use an action interrupt only when the action changes the relationship, the rhythm, or the information.
- Keep after-dialogue action beats only when they identify the speaker, show attitude, or change the scene.
- Avoid using “so-and-so said, so-and-so asked, so-and-so answered” in a row; omit them when the speaker is clear.
- Do not add stuttering, verbal tics, dialect, or wisecracks without basis. If the source character does not stutter, do not invent “th-this”.

IV. Rein in metaphor and performance

- In each scene, keep only a few metaphors that truly fit the character’s experience and add touch or danger.
- A new metaphor must come from the era, occupation, region, objects, and immediate environment the character knows. Characters will not use poetic or technological metaphors outside their knowledge.
- When the source already has an accurate, distinctive image, keep it first. Do not swap it for a flashier vehicle just to “defamiliarize”.
- Delete the second half that explains what a metaphor means. If the reader can see it, stop there.
- Ordinary movement, observation, and dialogue do not need “suddenly, instantly, fiercely, tightly, slowly” stacked in rotation.
- Keep the original hook at the chapter end. Close on a concrete action, discovery, choice, or threat. Do not add extra elevation about fate, the age, a storm, and the like.

V. Keep web-novel information that works

- Payoffs must be clear, and reactions must have layers, but do not say “he is strong, the crowd is shocked, this matter is hugely important” three times.
- Cultivation, combat, crafting, and system information must let the reader see the process; do not delete key steps in pursuit of a literary feel.
- Technique names, realm names, item names, numbers, durations, distances, quantities, and causal conditions must be checked against the source item by item.
- Suspense and information gaps already in the source must be kept. Do not explain the answer early.
- Concrete objects such as chopsticks, tea bowls, thresholds, herbs, weapons, and cloth must not be generalized into “thing, item, utensil”; life detail and cultural detail must not be deleted in pursuit of brevity.
- If speculative words such as “should, seems, as if, perhaps, looks like” express the character’s limit of knowledge, they must be kept. Do not turn speculation into fact on your own.

VI. Protect the source register

- First judge each sentence’s register. Solemn, classical, or literary sentences only get obvious errors fixed; neutral written language may have word order tuned; everyday speech may only get limited adjustment by the character’s identity.
- Do not casually replace words with a clear force or register color, such as “struggle, resist, refuse, gaze, stagger”, with “fight, go against, don’t, look, walk”.
- Do not colloquialize for the sake of colloquial, and do not literarize for the sake of literary. After polish, the sentence must still sound like the same author and the same character speaking.
- Core nouns, core verbs, and distinctive adjectives must not get meaningless synonym swaps.

【Mandatory rewrite rules】

When the following patterns appear, they must be handled. The right-hand side is a rewrite direction, not a demand to swap in a fixed sentence mechanically.

1. “not A, but B” / “it is not A, but B” / “not A, and not B, it is C”
   → State B or C directly; if A is necessary information, split it into two natural sentences.

2. “not only A, but also / still more B” / “this is not merely A”
   → Delete the progressive formula and write by the real cause or the order of actions.

3. “In this moment, he finally understood / realized / knew”
   → Write the concrete judgment he reaches; if the context is already clear, delete the whole sentence.

4. “the gears of fate” / “the tide of the age” / “a storm is coming” / “the story has only just begun”
   → Delete, and close with a concrete threat, goal, arrival, sound, or discovery that already exists in this chapter.

5. “as if the whole world” / “time seemed to freeze” / “the air seemed to solidify” / “everything around disappeared”
   → Change to a sound, action, or brief pause that can be perceived on the spot; if it is not needed, delete it outright.

6. “a trace of … flashed in the eyes / pupils” / “the corner of the mouth hooked a curve” / “a trace of … surfaced on the face”
   → Change respectively to a shift of gaze, a smile / cold smile / a tug at the mouth, a concrete expression; do not reuse the substitute sentence over and over in the same chapter.

7. “a stir in the heart” / “a shock in the chest” / “said inwardly” / “could not help / could not stop …”
   → Write the concrete thought or the direct action; if there is no new information, delete it.

8. “drew a sharp breath” / “pupils shrank” / “the whole body shook” / “clenched a fist” / “breath caught”
   → In principle keep the same kind of body reaction only once in a chapter; change the rest, by scene, into concrete reactions such as stopping a hand, looking at the wrong place, leaking half a sentence, stepping back, or avoiding a gaze.

9. “obviously, without question, needless to say, as one can imagine, it is worth mentioning, you should know”
   → Delete the lead-in and write the fact; if the fact does not exist, delete the whole sentence.

10. “seems, as if, as though” appearing densely
    → Keep them when they express real uncertainty; change purely decorative metaphors to direct writing. In principle no more than one per natural paragraph.

11. Three sentences in a row using “he + action”
    → Merge one of them, change where the information lands, or omit the subject once when there is no ambiguity; do not use a broken inversion.

12. Three adjectives, emotion words, or synonymous actions in a row
    → Keep the most accurate one or two, unless the three each carry different plot information.

13. “some unspeakable / hard-to-name / complicated emotion”
    → From context, change it to a concrete worry, desire, shame, hostility, or hesitation; if you cannot tell, show it with action and do not name it on your own.

14. “seems … but in fact …” / “on the surface … actually …”
    → Keep it only when it carries a real information gap; if it is only manufacturing a sense of cleverness, state the fact.

15. “meanwhile, next, the next second, just then, however, at this moment” serving as paragraph openings in a row
    → Keep only connectives that truly mark time or a turn; join the rest through action.

16. “he began …” / “he attempted …” / “he instinctively …”
    → If they do not carry start, failure, or unconscious meaning, write the action; if those meanings affect the plot, they must be kept.

17. “was silent for a moment” / “did not answer” / “did not pick up the line” / “quiet for an instant” appearing again and again
    → Keep them only when the silence itself changes the scene; otherwise write the concrete consequence of the silence, or go straight to the next action. Do not mechanically replace them with “dead silence”.

18. “knuckles whitened, nails dug into the palm, unconscious rubbing, lips pressed tight, brow tip slightly raised, Adam’s apple rolled” appearing densely
    → Keep the one that truly fits the character and scene; show the rest through whole posture, choice of action, way of speaking, or interaction with the environment.

19. “the gaze lingered three seconds, walked seven steps, heart beat three times, looked over it several times”
    → Keep exact numbers when they have setting or tactical meaning; when the character could not be counting, rewrite as one perception and what it discovers. Do not invent substitute stock such as “a moment”.

20. Isolated fragments such as “Hot.” / “Tired.” / “Slightly burning, the jade plate.”
    → Key landing short sentences may be kept; fragments missing subject and predicate, hanging in sense, or only a normal sentence chopped apart must be put back and completed.

21. High-frequency decorative words such as “seep, vaulted dome, glazed glass, gilt, gold-inlaid, haze, fine-grained, hum, silence, explosive sound, barely perceptible, whimper, nod, muffled grunt, tightly, forcibly, suddenly”
    → Do not delete blindly. Check whether they fit the genre, whether they provide irreplaceable information, and whether they repeat in the same chapter; rewrite when they are pure decoration or dense recurrence; keep them when they carry accurate setting and sensory information.

22. High-frequency metaphor vehicles such as “like a harp string, like a keyhole, like a stethoscope, like a receding tide, like spreading ink, each word like a nail”
    → Keep them when the source truly has scene basis; when they are all-purpose metaphors, switch to the character’s present experience or write the physical effect directly.

【Practices you must not learn wrongly from samples】

- Do not manufacture “human flavor” through a lot of object-fronting and predicate-delaying.
- Do not force normal short sentences into long comma-chains.
- Do not add a modal particle to every line of dialogue, and do not add stuttering to a normal character out of nowhere.
- Do not delete necessary detail that establishes space, causality, danger, and the cultivation process.
- Do not flatten every vivid but accurate sentence; what you remove is the template feel, not the writing.
- Do not sacrifice readability, character consistency, and grammatical correctness for the reason of “lowering the AI-detection rate”.
- Do not carry out mechanical word swaps unrelated to the genre, for example unifying “terminal” into “phone”, or unifying “glide” into “walk”. Whether a term is changed is decided only by the current world setting and the original meaning.
- Do not generalize the source’s concrete description into abstract words, and do not delete the character’s process of observing, trying, and judging for the sake of tightness.

9. Whether there is register downgrade, a character knowing beyond their knowledge, exact timing without basis, or repeated perception.
10. Randomly sample three changes: if the new sentence is only prettier than the original, and not more accurate or more natural, restore the original sentence.
